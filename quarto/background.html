Clinical background

Author

Vagish Hemmige

This page is for readers who want the clinical context before the methods. It summarizes what is known about cryptococcosis after kidney transplant and where the study sits among earlier work.

The infection

Cryptococcosis is an infection with the yeast Cryptococcus, usually C. neoformans, which is inhaled from the environment. In people with weakened immunity it can stay in the lungs or spread, most importantly to the brain and its linings as meningitis1,2. Treatment of meningitis and disseminated disease starts with an induction phase of amphotericin B with flucytosine, a lipid formulation of amphotericin B for transplant recipients, followed by fluconazole1–3.

After organ transplant

Among invasive fungal infections in solid organ transplant recipients, cryptococcosis is the third most common, after invasive candidiasis and aspergillosis; in a US surveillance network of transplant centres it made up 8% of invasive fungal infections2,4. Unlike most infections after transplant it tends to come late: the median time to onset was 575 days across organs in that network, and 616 days in kidney recipients in a large US hospital-records study4,5.

Which form the infection takes depends partly on immunosuppression. In a review of published cases, recipients on tacrolimus were less likely to have central nervous system involvement6, and over time transplant recipients have more often presented with disease confined to the lungs7.

Outcomes

Reported mortality varies with the era, the population and how long people are followed. In a prospective multicentre study of 111 transplant recipients, 14% had died at 90 days8; an earlier review of published cases found an overall death rate of 42%6. Among kidney recipients alone, a French cohort found 32.8% mortality at 12 months9 and a Brazilian cohort 38.3% at 30 days10.

Several factors have been associated with death:

  • Kidney failure at presentation, in both the case review and the prospective study6,8.
  • Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, cyclosporine): associated with lower mortality in the prospective study8.
  • Liver disease: end-stage liver disease was a strong predictor of early death in cryptococcosis in general11, and mortality in people with cirrhosis has been high12.

Not every study agrees. In the French kidney cohort, mortality did not differ by whether patients had meningitis9. Compared with patients who had neither HIV nor a transplant, transplant recipients have had similar or lower mortality, in a single-centre study and in US insurance-claims and health-record data13–15.

Earlier studies with national data

Earlier USRDS studies looked at how often fungal infections occurred after kidney transplant and what predicted them, grouping cryptococcosis with other fungi or modelling only the risk of infection; neither studied death after cryptococcosis16–18. One of them found maintenance tacrolimus associated with more hospitalizations for fungal infection16; that was about how often infection occurred, not about death. Studies of US hospital records that did single out cryptococcosis were small for transplant recipients: 158 cases across all organs in one5,19.

Fiorica et al. studied 1,265 kidney transplant recipients hospitalized with cryptococcosis from 2006 to 2016, which the authors describe as, to their knowledge, the largest such cohort studied over a 10-year period, and found that 22% had died within 6 months18. This project rebuilds that analysis. No study was found that checks how accurately billing codes identify cryptococcosis (see Data sources), so cases here are cases by code, not by laboratory confirmation.
