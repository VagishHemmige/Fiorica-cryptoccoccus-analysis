The question and the design in brief

The study asks how many kidney transplant recipients die within 6 months of being hospitalized with cryptococcosis, and which characteristics are associated with dying. It follows recipients from their first cryptococcosis hospitalization after transplant. It compares those who died within 6 months with those who survived, so it describes associations rather than causes.

This project rebuilds the published study from its paper and supplement, and the paper is the specification. Where the paper is silent, the choice is made openly in a decision issue rather than borrowed from a later reconstruction of the original code.

Specified in: Scope, §1 Objective, §2 Design.

Finding the cryptococcosis hospitalizations

A hospitalization counts if a cryptococcosis code appears in any of its diagnosis positions, not only the principal diagnosis. The stays come from the USRDS hospitalization files. These are the inpatient part of the Medicare institutional claims, already organised by stay, and they carry the details the study needs: ICU days, procedures and discharge status. Hospital records that overlap in time are treated as one stay. A stay counts only if it began on or after the first transplant: a stay that began before it, even if it continued past the transplant, is cryptococcosis before transplant, which the paper excludes.

Alternatives considered:

  • Medicare institutional claims instead of the hospitalization files. Claims are the raw billing records; the hospitalization files are built from them and already grouped into stays. Using claims would exercise more of the usRds package, but it would add nothing as a check, because the USRDS Researcher’s Guide describes the hospitalization files as a subset of the claims. Not done (#1).
  • Which code set applies around October 2015. A stay that straddles the switch from ICD-9 to ICD-10 is coded by its discharge date, which is Medicare’s rule; its admission date would be the other choice (#8).
  • Where the code lists come from. The paper’s supplement and the 2025 reconstruction list the same codes, with a few gaps: neither lists codes for ventriculoperitoneal shunt, which the paper reports, so that list was rebuilt from the official code sets; and the reconstruction’s diabetes codes, read literally, would miss most ICD-10 diabetes codes, so they are matched as open prefixes, as the supplement does (#8).

Specified in: §3 Data source, §5.

Who is included

The cohort is adults aged 18 to 90 with a kidney or kidney–pancreas transplant whose first cryptococcosis hospitalization came after their transplant. People who had a cryptococcosis claim before their first transplant are excluded. The paper’s cohort is described as having Medicare as primary insurance. Because every index stay is a Medicare-paid record, no separate payer check is applied. An explicit check is described as a possible variation.

A cryptococcosis claim before the first transplant excludes the person: a hospital record or a physician’s bill, and one is enough. Earlier claims can only be seen while someone had Medicare, which recipients on dialysis usually did and those transplanted before dialysis often did not.

Alternatives considered:

  • Checking Medicare as primary payer. The paper’s cohort had Medicare as primary insurance but the paper gives no time window. One could require it on the admission date, for the year before, or from transplant onward; a longer window makes claims-based measures more complete and shrinks the cohort. Here no explicit check is made, and the check is kept as a variation (#3).
  • A stay that begins before the first transplant and ends after it. The reconstruction kept such stays, with time since transplant set to zero. The paper excludes cryptococcosis before transplant, so here they are excluded (#4).
  • How many earlier claims to require, and of what kind. Counting only hospital stays would miss cryptococcosis treated outside hospital. Requiring two or more claims would spare people with a single rule-out code, the way many studies define a case. For excluding an earlier condition, though, one claim is the usual rule, and a clinician billing for cryptococcosis before transplant is itself a reason to doubt that the later episode is new (#4;1).
  • Alaska and Hawaii. They could be counted in the West census region; here they are grouped with the territories as “Outside”, as in the reconstruction, because they are hard to compare with the mainland for a study like this. No one is excluded for where they live (#12).

Specified in: §4 Study population, §9.

What is measured

Characteristics come from three places:

  • the index stay itself: meningitis or not, acute kidney injury, diabetes, cirrhosis, ICU admission and procedures;
  • USRDS and UNOS transplant records: transplant history, BMI, pretransplant dialysis, HIV and CMV status, rejection;
  • earlier hospitalizations: transplant complications.

For someone with more than one transplant, the registry information comes from their most recent transplant before the cryptococcosis hospitalization, because that is the graft and drug regimen they were living with. Acute rejection counts any recorded episode, treated or not, at that transplant or at a follow-up visit before the hospitalization. The registry is the source; hospital claims coded for rejection are an alternative the plan describes but does not use.

Immunosuppressive drugs come from the transplant registry records (UNOS) held in USRDS, at transplant and at the first follow-up visit after transplant, as the paper’s Methods describe. The paper also mentions billing codes; those may be revisited as the source (#2).

Decision pending: whether physician (CPT) billing codes for procedures are used as well as the hospital’s ICD procedure codes (#5).

Alternatives considered:

  • Which transplant’s records to use. The reconstruction used the first transplant. For someone transplanted more than once, the most recent transplant before the hospitalization describes the graft and drugs they had at the time, so that one is used (#12).
  • Acute rejection from the registry or from claims. The registry records any episode at transplant or follow-up. Hospital claims coded for kidney transplant rejection are the alternative, but ICD-9 has no code specific to rejection, so a claims definition would only work from October 2015 (#12; plan §9).
  • Immunosuppression from the registry or from billing codes. The paper’s Methods mention billing codes, and its supplement lists drug billing codes for cyclosporine and tacrolimus; the reconstruction used the registry. Billing codes would show drugs actually dispensed, but only for some drugs and only where Medicare paid. The registry is used, at transplant and at the first follow-up visit, as the paper’s Methods describe. The paper’s Discussion speaks of a “1-year follow-up”; the Methods’ “first post-transplant visit” is followed (#2).

Specified in: §5.

Outcome and analysis

The main outcome is death from any cause within 180 days of admission. The methods follow the paper:

  • survivors and non-survivors are compared;
  • survival over time is drawn as a Kaplan–Meier curve;
  • the factors associated with death are found with logistic regression, using backward elimination as the paper did.

The paper’s own final model is also refitted exactly as published, with its eight characteristics, so its odds ratios can be compared with the published ones directly (Table 4).

Alternatives considered:

  • Rerunning the selection, or fitting the published model. Rerunning backward elimination repeats what the paper did, but a small change in the data can change which characteristics survive, and then the odds ratios cannot be compared one for one. Fitting the paper’s eight characteristics directly compares the estimates, but not the selection. Both are done, and Table 4 shows them side by side.
  • Backward elimination at all. Selecting variables by their P values gives P values that look smaller than they should and a model that may not repeat in other data. Choosing the variables beforehand on clinical grounds, or using penalized regression, are the usual alternatives. Elimination is kept because the aim is to repeat the paper; its limits are listed in plan §11.
  • How each step is tested. A characteristic with several categories (such as region) gets one joint test, not one per category, and every step uses the same people: everyone with all candidates known. This is how Stata, the paper’s software, does it. Testing with likelihood ratios, or letting the sample grow as variables are removed, are reasonable alternatives that can give a different final model.

Specified in: §7 Outcomes, §8 Statistical analysis.
